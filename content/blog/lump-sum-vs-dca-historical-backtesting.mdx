Suppose you already have money set aside for a long-term investment. You could buy an ETF today or invest a fixed amount at regular intervals. A lump sum vs. dollar-cost averaging backtest compares those decisions on the same price history. Give each strategy the same starting budget, the same holding, and the same ending date. Then count any cash still waiting to be invested. That cash belongs to the investor and changes both the balance and its response to a falling market.

## Give both strategies the same cash budget

Choose a deployment schedule before examining the historical result. For example, an illustrative $12,000 budget can fund an immediate $12,000 purchase or twelve $1,000 purchases. In the staged account, each purchase converts cash into shares. It adds no new wealth on the purchase date. Record any interest earned on cash and use the same assumptions for fees, dividends, and taxes on both sides.

Investing part of every paycheck answers a different question. The later installments only become available when paid. A spreadsheet that credits all future paychecks as starting cash gives the immediate purchase an advantage the investor never had. FINRA discusses how spreading an available lump sum differs from investing money as you earn it. [FINRA on dollar-cost averaging](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/dollar-cost-averaging)

> **Takeaway:** Match the amount of money available and when it becomes available. A larger early stock position will naturally move more with the market.

## Test what happens around a bear-market entry

- **Decline soon after entry.** The immediate position absorbs the downturn on the whole budget. Staging retains cash and can buy more shares in later installments at lower prices.
- **Fast rebound after a decline.** Early purchases participate in the rebound. Cash that has yet to be deployed misses part of it, so a favorable average purchase price alone tells you little about the ending balance.
- **Rally before a slow decline.** Staging may purchase several installments at elevated prices. If the decline comes after deployment, both approaches can have substantial exposure by then.

Choose entry dates using a rule you could have applied in real time or label historical turning points as stress tests. When you have access to custom dates, repeat the exercise before multiple downturns and during rising markets. A specific trough selected with hindsight can illustrate the effect of timing without estimating how often either strategy would prevail.

### Distinguish cash drag from volatility drag

Cash drag is the performance difference from holding some assets outside the market. It can help during falling prices and reduce participation during rising prices. Volatility drag describes compound returns. In an illustrative two-period sequence, a 20% rise followed by a 20% decline leaves $100 at $96. The arithmetic average is zero, while the compounded result is a loss. Staging reduces equity exposure during deployment. Once deployed, the account experiences the volatility of its holdings.

Behavioral risk also matters. If an immediate drop would lead you to abandon an allocation, the loss and recovery path merits as much attention as the endpoint. A staged strategy has its own test of discipline. Future purchases must still happen when headlines are discouraging or prices seem too high.

## FAQ

### Does lump sum always beat dollar-cost averaging?

No. The ranking depends on the price path and the return on uninvested cash. A decline during deployment can favor staged entry. A sustained rise can favor investing the entire amount early.

### How do I compare the strategies fairly?

Start both accounts with the same available cash. Apply the same asset, time window, and cost assumptions, and include unspent cash when measuring the staged account’s value.

### Can DCA eliminate a bear-market loss?

DCA spreads purchases and changes how much money is exposed during deployment. Existing shares still lose value when the market falls. A fully invested DCA account remains exposed to further declines.
